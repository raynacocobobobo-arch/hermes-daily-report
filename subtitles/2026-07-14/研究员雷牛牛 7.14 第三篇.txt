I'm so done! What a garbage index, the Hang Seng Tech! This private message to Niuniu from a long time fan was overflowing with anger and helplessness. What completely enraged this fan, and hundreds of thousands of other Hang Seng Tech fund holders online, was the recent quarterly rebalance announcement from Hang Seng Indexes Company. What should have been a routine constituent stock adjustment instead sparked a tsunami of public outcry throughout the investment world. The gist of the announcement was that the Hang Seng Tech Index would ruthlessly drop Kingdee International and Kingsoft, which had fallen to low prices. Instead, it's forcefully adding two newcomers, Zhipu AI and MINI Max, who've led the AI large model race this year with soaring valuations. The news sparked instant outrage and despair across financial communities, with investors once again lashing out at the Hang Seng Tech Index online. Honestly, it's no wonder investors are furious. This generation of Hang Seng Tech holders has really suffered. Looking back at this tech rally, global tech stocks, led by US giants NVIDIA and Apple, have been surging ahead, hitting new highs left and right. But for the holders of the Hang Seng Tech Index, in this so called tech bull market, they could only watch helplessly as their assets kept plummeting. completely missed out on this entire epic AI bull run. During the value stocks bull run, they called the Hang Seng Tech Index tech; but during the tech bull run, it's just food delivery. Having endured massive losses, value stock holders now see the index inflicting further damage on their meager remaining capital. Meanwhile, Zhipu AI and MINI Max, ever since their IPOs, have seen their stock prices surge wildly, fueled by their high flying AI narratives. Even more absurdly, on the day the rebalance was announced, Zhipu AI and MINI Max's stock prices soared yet again. posting staggering single day gains of twenty six percent and fifteen percent respectively, which became the last straw for investors. People took to social media, furiously denouncing it—this wasn't an index rebalance; it was nothing short of legal, compliant robbery, bailing out high priced stocks and tech speculators. Buying into bubbles at their peak, then selling off old components at their steepest lows. So basically, they missed out on all the tech gains, but didn't skip a single bill for the tech bubble. Actually, when it comes to buying high, the Hang Seng Tech Index is a repeat offender. That's because the Hang Seng Tech Index has a critical underlying rule: no listing time limits. If a company's market cap is big enough, it gets quickly added. As a result, many mega giants IPO'ing at a bull market's peak were swiftly indexed right after listing, leaving fund investors holding the bag at the top. The most classic case is Kuaishou, which remains the most heavily criticized move in the history of the Hang Seng Tech Index. Kuaishou listed in Hong Kong on the fifth of february , twenty twenty one, as the first short video stock. It was the very peak of Hong Kong's internet bubble. After its IPO, Kuaishou's stock price soared to a record high of four hundred and seventeen Hong Kong dollars, topping one point seven trillion in market cap. Given its massive market cap, the Hang Seng Tech Index fast tracked its inclusion, adding Kuaishou as a constituent merely eighteen days after its IPO. As a result, ETF's were mandated to buy heavily around four hundred HKD, effectively buying at the peak. Then came the internet anti monopoly crackdown and industry slowdown, sending Kuaishou into a bear market, its stock price plummeting to thirty one Hong Kong dollars, a fall of over ninety percent. To this day, they are still paying the price for that eighteen day impulse. So why does the Hang Seng Tech Index so frequently put on this seemingly absurd show of buying high? The core issue lies in its index rules: selecting Hong Kong's top thirty tech companies by market cap. Plus, it has what's called a 'fast track inclusion' mechanism. According to the rules, as long as a newly listed tech stock's market cap can time jump into the top ten of the sector at a specific point in time, or crosses a specific high threshold, it can bypass the traditional observation period and be fast tracked into the index constituents in the latest rebalance. And this time, Zhipu AI and MINI Max perfectly triggered this mechanism. Amidst the AI large model boom and the frenzy of short term institutional capital pouring in, They have indeed become behemoths that cannot be ignored among the top thirty tech companies in the Hong Kong stock market. At this point, the Hang Seng Indexes Company has no choice; the system can only passively execute its program. If the index company were to reject them on the grounds that their valuation is too steep—say, a P/E ratio in the hundreds—it would be openly violating its own fundamental rules. The index would then lose objectivity in reflecting the overall Hong Kong tech industry. This frequent 'bag holding' phenomenon seems to many investors like a garbage rule tailor made for all sorts of VC tycoons and short term market manipulators to cash out at the top. But in reality, this intuition isn't objective. In global capital markets, whether it's the S and P five hundred, CSI three hundred, or the surging ChiNext Index, the world's top tier, broad based market indices with excellent long term returns. The core logic behind how they select stocks and allocate positions is mostly this seemingly dumb market cap weighting mechanism. In other words, whoever has a larger market cap gets included, and the bigger that cap, the higher their weighting. Plus, pretty much all of them have faced flak for 'buying high'. At the end of twenty twenty, the S and P five hundred saw its largest and most controversial forced rebalance ever: bringing Tesla on board at its peak. What was Tesla's status then? Over the past year, global investment institutions went on a buying frenzy, pushing Tesla's stock price up a staggering seven hundred percent within just one year. By December, just before its official inclusion, Tesla's P/E ratio had reached an astonishing thousand. What does a P/E of thousand even mean? Any value investor who took one look would say this is a serious Ponzi scheme. Many conservative Wall Street fund managers were publicly slamming the S and P five hundred, warning it was throwing billions of dollars into the fire. Tesla's market cap surpassed that of several major traditional automakers combined. This was a textbook case of buying at the absolute peak. But by both market cap and earnings report standards, Tesla met the requirements. So, even though the S and P five hundred had the power of human review, they ultimately had to hold their noses and add Tesla, forcing major index funds to frantically snap up shares in huge volumes. In the final minutes of that day, Tesla's trading volume for the entire session exploded to a record high. After its index inclusion, Tesla’s stock fluctuated sharply, dropping nearly forty percent. And as for what happened next, well, you all probably know the story: that peak in valuation back then, looking back now, was just the halfway point up the mountain. The stock price soared, eventually peaking at nearly five hundred dollars. Nice! Now, I'm sure many of you watching are going to push back, saying, 'Hey, the S and P five hundred requires four consecutive quarters of profit—there's a fundamental floor!' The Hang Seng Tech Index has no profit requirement—that's a huge bug. How can you compare them? But this very thing is a double edged sword—it's both a flaw of the Hang Seng Tech Index and its greatest strength. What if the S and P five hundred mirrored Hang Seng Tech, only size based, no profit threshold? So, what would have happened in Tesla's case? Because of its strict profitability requirements, the S and P five hundred kept Tesla out in the cold for many years. Even if it had been included just one year earlier—say, at the end of twenty nineteen—Tesla's Shanghai factory had already broken ground by then. The Model three was also starting to show signs of dominating the U S market, expectations had improved, and its market cap already met large cap standards. but because its financial metrics, based on the latest quarterly and cumulative four quarter profits, didn't meet standards, the S and P Index Committee could only watch from the sidelines, helplessly. If Tesla had been added to the S and P five hundred back in twenty nineteen, when its stock price was around thirteen dollars, then in just six years, that price would have skyrocketed thirty eight fold. Similar cases have frequently occurred in A shares, with CATL being the prime example. In september twenty eighteen, The ChiNext Index used a fast track process to swiftly add CATL— which had just gone public barely three months prior—into its core constituents. and even assigned it an incredibly massive weighting. Back then, the market reacted with widespread scorn and doubt, exactly like today’s Hang Seng Tech investors berating Zhipu AI and MINI Max. First off, you have to understand the backdrop of the twenty eighteen market: A shares were going through a brutal bear market driven by deleveraging and China US trade friction. ChiNext plummeted to a desperate thousand two hundred points, and veteran tech stocks' P/E ratios were generally crushed to just over ten times. CATL, a hot IPO, exploded nearly two hundred percent, hitting a one hundred sixty billion RMB market cap with a P/E over fifty. The market was widely bearish. A battery maker at fifty times P/E in this market?! Are you kidding me? Such a high weighting, they might as well rename the ChiNext Market the 'Ningde Board'! Purely surviving on subsidies. Once the lock up expires, the ChiNext Index will be the ultimate bagholder. And CATL, single handedly, propelled the ChiNext Index to record highs. So why is there such a huge outcry over the Hang Seng Tech Index's latest new constituents? Yet, under the same selection rules, the ChiNext Market, which also often buys at peaks, is highly praised—even seen as hitting the mark perfectly. The reason is actually quite simple: it's mainly due to the Hang Seng Tech Index's recent sustained plunge. In the capital market, going up is the only justice, while going down is the original sin. When an index is in a prolonged, one way decline, or stuck in that dead time at the bottom, holders' emotions are already extremely tense and sensitive. At this point, any tiny tweak to the index, in the eyes of holders who've been crushed by losses, gets blown up into a malicious backstab and a capitalist conspiracy. Drop old software stocks, they'll accuse you of selling at the bottom, just before a rebound. Add new AI tech darlings, they'll accuse you of buying high, benefiting insiders. As that internet catchphrases goes: when someone hates you, even your breathing is wrong. But if we cast our minds back to early twenty twenty one, when the Hong Kong stock market was soaring, did anyone call the Hang Seng Tech Index trash? No! Back then, four to five percent daily surges were common for the Hang Seng Tech Index, lauded as the 'Eastern Nasdaq'. All fund investors were shouting: 'To Hong Kong, to seize pricing power!' Even back then, when the index snapped up Kuaishou at its four hundred yuan peak, everyone just saw it as the index's sharp eye for tracking unicorns. Remember twenty twenty five, when Hong Kong tech stocks, AI fueled, led worldwide gains? Back when Xiaomi and Tencent were dancing like elephants, did anyone call the Hang Seng Tech Index a bagholder? Neither did that happen. Back then, people praised the "strong get stronger" capitalization weighted index, believing its rock solid approach of holding top stocks fearlessly was the only way to big money. But once the market turns from a rally to a slump and the index starts bleeding and pulling back, that very same logic instantly becomes the root of all evil, condemned across the internet. On the other hand, the ChiNext Market, over the past few major cycles, it successfully used the new energy and Hard and Core Tech boom to deliver significant wealth effects and record net asset values. With the filter of that wealth effect in place, even when it made the same kind of hasty, quick inclusions, Even with the same 'bag holding' costs, people would subjectively view it heroically—supporting indigenous Hard and Core Technology and catching the industry's early rise. This uproar over the latest Hang Seng Tech Index inclusion isn't really about the toxicity of Zhipu AI and MINI Max, or the flaws of market cap weighting. It was simply hundreds of thousands of Hang Seng Tech Index investors, having lost their shirts, using this rebalancing as a collective opportunity to vent their despair. This logic isn't perfect; it's a double edged sword with inherent, irreconcilable huge advantages and disadvantages. Its downside is what you're seeing today: buying in at the peak. Whenever the market is caught in a frenzy of irrational hype, it will inevitably act as an amplifier for that bubble. forcing you, at peak valuations, to carry the bags for VC whales and short term manipulators.
